1 How would you evaluate the performance of the agents on each ticket, and why?

#101Bulldog tee — found the real problem, then forgot the customer

Tauhid asked for one white tee in size S. The shop has ten white tees — but none of them are size S. The Ticket 101 tab shows Inventory checking the exact size and finding zero, which is the whole game on this ticket: an agent that had only asked "do we have white tees?" would have said yes and promised a tee that does not exist.

It then found the second problem. The only shop that prints apparel for Campus Customs is Bulldog Print Co, and Bulldog Print Co refuses to send anything while an old bill of $840.00 sits unpaid. So the tee could not be reordered until that bill was settled. Accounting did the best single thing in the whole run here: before recommending the $840.00, it looked up the rent that was also about to come due, to check the shop could afford both. You can see that in the tool list on the Ticket 101 tab — it called get_lease_rent_due on a ticket that has nothing to do with rent.

What the human was asked to decide: Accounting put one request in front of the owner — APR-8AF97D77, $840.00 to settle Bulldog Print Co's bill. The agents did not and could not pay it themselves. It was approved by Purnima Prasad, and only then did the money leave: you can see the balance fall from $3,400.00 to $2,560.00 on the Cash tab. Had it been declined, nothing would have moved and the ticket would have been refused instead.

The failure nobody wrote to Tauhid. The shop spent $840.00 partly on his behalf and sent him not one word. The agents had a tool for writing to customers and simply did not use it on this ticket.

In fairness the tee could never have reached Tauhid today. Once the bill was paid, the next step was to order the reprint — and nobody built a tool for placing an order with a vendor. That limit is mine, not theirs. The ticket is marked resolved because the shop has run out of moves, not because the order was filled, and the ticket note says exactly that.

#102Rent — the best work of the three

The landlord's email said "rent due in 2 days". The Ticket 102 tab shows Facilities refusing to take that at face value: it opened the actual lease and confirmed the real figure ($2,400.00) and the real date (2026-09-02), then reported that the rent was coming but not yet late. That is the difference between believing someone who is owed money and checking the paperwork.

Accounting then confirmed the shop could afford it and raised one request for the owner — APR-668147AF, $2,400.00 — paying nothing until a person signed. It was approved by Purnima Prasad. The Cash tab shows the result: $2,400.00 out, taking the shop from $2,560.00 to $160.00. All three tickets are closed, but this is the only one where closing it also means the job is genuinely done — the landlord has the money.

Why this one worked everything it needed was something the shop could actually do. Paying rent requires no goods to exist.

#103Discount — good business sense, poor customer care

The Yale AI Club asked for 20 navy hoodies at a discount. The Ticket 103 tab shows Customer Service looking up what the shop paid for a hoodie ($22.00) before naming any price at all. That is the right instinct — without it you can talk yourself into a discount that loses money on every item. It suggested $46.00, which is 21% off the normal $58.00 and still leaves the shop $24.00 per hoodie.

What the human was asked to decide: APR-D85EDD3A — sell at $46.00 a hoodie instead of $58.00. It was approved by Purnima Prasad and moved $0.00, because agreeing a price is not a purchase. The Cash tab shows this line at zero while the other two tickets show money leaving. Nothing was bought and the shop's normal $58.00 price was left untouched for everyone else.

The failure there are only 8 hoodies in that size, not 20. The price was put in front of the owner for all 20 as if finding the other 12 were a detail to sort out later, and nothing was written to the Yale AI Club explaining the shortfall. Agreeing a price for goods you do not have is a promise waiting to break.

Overall — the three human approvals in one place: ticket 101 raised APR-8AF97D77 for $840.00 (approved, cash moved), ticket 102 raised APR-668147AF for $2,400.00 (approved, cash moved), and ticket 103 raised APR-D85EDD3A for a $46.00 price (approved, no cash moved). That is one decision per ticket, every one of them taken by a person and none by an agent. The handling of money was flawless: look at the Cash tab — the agents paid for nothing on their own, both payments happened only after a signature, and the final balance adds up exactly. The repeated weakness is people: two customers were waiting, and neither was written to.

3 What would have been simpler with one agent with tools — and why?

Honestly, most of it. Here is what the three runs on this page actually cost:

Every hand-off starts a whole second conversation. The other agent has to be told the situation from scratch, look things up, think, and report back — and all of that reading and writing is what you pay for. Ticket 101 needed 262,000 words of reading and writing to reach a conclusion one agent could have reached using the same 28 tools, because the Boss already has access to every tool the specialists have. On these three tickets, asking a specialist bought no new ability — only extra time and cost.

One agent would also have been far simpler to build. There would be no rules about who may ask whom, no limit on how many times agents can pass work around, and no risk of them talking in circles. The history on each ticket tab would read as one straight story instead of several overlapping ones. Several of the worst bugs in this project existed only because there were five agents: a spending limit meant for one agent was accidentally applied to the whole team and stopped every run; the Boss crashed whenever a specialist asked it a question back, because it replies in a different format; and an agent that had been asked for help showed on screen as "not called" because its work was not written down until it finished.

The important point the five agents are not what keeps the money safe. Nothing stops a payment because Accounting is a separate agent. Payments are stopped by the tools themselves: no payment runs without a signature, each signature works only once and only for that exact bill, the amount is read from the shop's records rather than typed by the AI, and anything that would overdraw the account is refused outright. One agent using those same tools would be exactly as safe.

What five agents did genuinely buy is smaller but real: each one only had to be taught the rules for its own job, so the discount instructions were not cluttered with rent rules — and you can see who reached which conclusion, which is why the ticket tabs on this page read the way they do. For a shop this size, that makes the work easier to explain, not more correct. Put simply: five agents are a better story of the work; one agent would have been a better machine for doing it.

4 Three new problems this team could solve with the tools already built

A. A customer order the shop can actually fill

A student orders a blue cap. Unlike the size S tee on Ticket 101, the shop has 40 of them. Every tool needed already exists: Inventory can check the exact size, Customer Service can check the price, there is a tool that takes items off the shelf (and refuses to take more than there are), another that writes the confirmation, and another that closes the ticket.

This is the case the board never got to show. All three tickets on this page are closed, but only #102 ended with anyone actually receiving what they asked for — the other two closed with goods still outstanding. A cap order would close and be delivered, and it needs nothing new at all.

B. A second supplier stops shipping over an unpaid bill

Elm City Gifts, who supply the mugs, send a bill that goes unpaid — and the shop has zero crest mugs left. The team can check whether that supplier is blocked, read what is owed and whether it is late, put the payment in front of the owner, and pay it once signed, which frees the supplier to ship again.

This is the same shape of problem as Ticket 101, with a different supplier and product. The team solved it once; the tools work again without changes.

C. Two bills arrive and the money only covers one

The team can read the balance, read each bill and its due date, see what has already been paid, lay out which one matters most, and ask the owner to approve one at a time. If anything would take the account below zero, the tool refuses it rather than trusting the AI to notice.

It nearly happened on this very run. The Cash tab shows the two bills totalled $3,240.00 against $3,400.00 — payable together, but with only $160.00 to spare. A slightly larger invoice and the shop would have had to choose.

5 Three new problems this team could not solve — and what it would take

A. Actually ordering the reprint

Why it fails today: this is the exact wall Ticket 101 hit. The $840.00 bill was paid and Bulldog Print Co became willing to print again — and then nothing could happen, because no tool exists for placing an order with a supplier. The team could see the door was open and had no way to walk through it. That is the real reason Ticket 101 says "Waiting on vendor" instead of "Resolved", and it would say that forever.

What to store: a list of orders placed (supplier, item, size, how many, price, date ordered, date expected) and a record of what actually turned up.

Tools needed: one to place an order — which must need a signature first, because placing an order commits the shop's money; one to check where an order has got to; and one to add stock when a delivery arrives. That last one should be the only way items can ever be added, so stock can never appear just because someone says it did.

Agents needed: none new. Inventory already handles suppliers and delivery times; this would let it actually place the order instead of only advising, with Accounting still controlling the spending.

B. Money coming in — sales and refunds

Why it fails today: in this shop money only ever goes out. Look at the Cash tab — every line is a payment, and the balance only ever falls. There is no way to record a sale, and no way to refund someone whose hoodie arrived with a hole in it. The $46.00 price approved on Ticket 103 would have brought in $920.00 if all 20 hoodies sold, and the shop could not have recorded a single cent of it.

What to store: a record of sales, and a record of refunds linked back to the sale they came from.

Tools needed: one that records a sale — adding the money and removing the items at the same moment, so the two can never disagree — and one that issues a refund, which must need a signature, since it is the easiest tool in the set to misuse.

Agents needed: Accounting would widen from only paying bills to also taking money in, and Customer Service would be allowed to recommend a refund while never being able to give one.

C. Time moving forward

Why it fails today: the shop is frozen on 2026-08-31. Every date on this page comes from that one day. The team can answer "is the rent late today?" — as Facilities did on Ticket 102 — but it cannot answer "what happens on 2 September if nobody pays?", and it cannot see next month's rent coming. Every judgement on this board is a single snapshot.

What to store: a list of bills that repeat (what, how often, next due), and a history of how late each bill has become.

Tools needed: one that moves the shop's date forward — which should never be given to an agent, because an agent that can change the date can make anything late or not late; one that lists what is coming up; and one that chases a bill that has gone properly overdue.

Agents needed: a sixth agent — a Planner — whose job is next week rather than today: what is due, whether the money will stretch, and what to put in front of the owner now so they are not deciding in a panic on the day. None of the current five ever look past today, because nothing in this shop ever has.
